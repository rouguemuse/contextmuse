Oak & Ember Kitchen

THE OPERATOR'S QUESTION
“Why are our discounts so high?”

The owner noticed discount expense climbed to 7.8% of sales last month. The Snapshot was hired to answer this specific question, prove the dollar amount, and identify the exact fix.

Two promotions are being stacked on the same guest bills

Priority 01 • Immediate Action
Finding ID: SNAP-01 Certainty: VERIFIED (Calculated from POS) How Hard to Fix: Very Easy (5-minute POS setting) Area: Discount Exclusivity
WHAT WE FOUND
Two discount programs—a 15% VIP guest discount and a $10 Happy Hour voucher—were applied simultaneously to the exact same 218 guest checks over the 30-day review period.
WHY IT MATTERS
Promotions were designed to bring in new diners or fill empty early tables, not give away margin twice. Stacking both discounts allowed high-spending parties to take more than 28% off their total bill, reducing net margin on prime dining hours without driving extra guest counts.
WHAT IT MAY BE COSTING
Verified in POS Records
$2,180.00 / 30d
218 checks × $10 overlapping voucher
Annualized Money Lost
$26,160.00 / yr
Direct profit loss if left unfixed
Recovery Speed
Immediate
Effective on next shift once toggled
HOW SURE ARE WE?
VERIFIED “We can calculate this directly from the records you provided.” Every dollar in this $2,180 finding comes from itemized check IDs where both discount button codes were recorded on the same receipt.
WHAT TO CHECK
For one Friday shift, go to the main bar terminal during the 5:00–7:00 PM Happy Hour transition. Test whether a server can apply the $10 Happy Hour voucher to a check that already has the 15% VIP discount applied without requiring a manager passcode.
WHAT TO DO NEXT
Open your POS backend manager portal under Discounts > Rules > Combinability. Set both the "15% VIP" discount and the "$10 HH Voucher" to "Exclusive." Once toggled, entering one promotion will automatically prevent or remove the other on the same bill.
ADDITIONAL ISSUE NOTICED • OUTSIDE THIS SNAPSHOT

While reviewing check-level logs, we noticed 4 kitchen modifications (extra bacon, side avocado, sub gluten-free bun) entered as unpriced kitchen notes across 340+ checks.

F-01: Extras are being added to pizzas but not charged on the check

Priority 01 • Quick Revenue Win
Finding ID: F-01 Certainty: MODELED (Estimate with Useful Range) How Hard to Fix: Very Easy (POS Button Addition) Area: Menu Modifiers
WHAT WE FOUND
About 14% of specialty pizza orders (780 tickets over 60 days) included an extra or substitution written into the kitchen notes—such as "extra mozzarella", "add pepperoni", or "sub gluten-free dough"—without any corresponding charge added to the guest check.
WHY IT MATTERS
The kitchen consumes the premium ingredients and prep time, but the bill never collects the money. Servers use freeform notes because they are faster than hunting through sub-menus.
WHAT IT MAY BE COSTING
~$1,840.00 / month ($22,080.00 / year). Estimated from an average $2.35 menu charge across 780 unbilled modifiers.
HOW SURE ARE WE?
MODELED “This is an estimate. We have enough information to calculate a useful range, but at least one assumption is involved.” We counted the exact note occurrences; the total dollar figure assumes standard modifier pricing.
WHAT TO CHECK
For one busy Friday dinner, compare 20 kitchen bump tickets with their corresponding final guest receipts. Check how many tickets show a topping note without a line-item price on the check.
WHAT TO DO NEXT
Create top-level modifier buttons on the main ordering screen for the 5 most frequent additions (Extra Mozz, Add Pepperoni, Sub GF Crust, Add Ricotta, Extra Crispy). Remove server permission to type freeform kitchen notes without manager override.

F-04: Staff meals are currently coded as manager comps

Priority 02 • Reporting Accuracy
Finding ID: F-04 Certainty: NEEDS VALIDATION (Policy Check Needed) How Hard to Fix: Very Easy (POS Button) Area: Comp Coding & Reporting
WHAT WE FOUND
$1,640 of transactions over 60 days sit inside the manager guest-recovery comp category. 92% of these comps occur strictly during shift change windows (3:30–4:30 PM and 10:30–11:00 PM) and consist of single-entree orders rung under manager approval passcodes.
WHY IT MATTERS
Current coding makes guest-recovery comp reporting unreliable. It appears that managers are comping food for dissatisfied diners when they are actually authorizing legitimate staff shift meals. This prevents the owner from knowing how much food is genuinely being given away to fix guest issues.
WHAT IT MAY BE COSTING
Unknown until staff-meal policy and allowable amount are confirmed. If staff were entitled to these meals, the food cost was already incurred as an employee benefit. Changing the POS button will not put cash back into the restaurant's bank account—it only corrects reporting accuracy.
HOW SURE ARE WE?
NEEDS VALIDATION “We found a real pattern, but we need another report or an in-store check before we can say why it is happening.” The timing and item patterns look identical to employee meals, but the GM must confirm whether staff meals are authorized.
WHAT TO CHECK
Ask your general manager whether line cooks and servers receive authorized shift meals and what button managers currently use to ring them in.
WHAT TO DO NEXT
Create a separate "Employee Shift Meal" non-revenue comp code in the POS. Set a daily cap (e.g. 1 entree per shift) so staff meals no longer distort guest-satisfaction comp reports.

Mercury Bar & Grill

THE KNOWN PROBLEM
“Labor rose from about 22% to 28%. Why?”

The owner already knew labor had drifted out of control by 6.1 percentage points ($4,850/mo excess cost). They did not need another broad scan of modifiers or drinks. The Deep Dive was hired to investigate only why labor increased, isolate the true root cause, and test the fix.

Root Cause: Closing shift duration doubled due to manager overlap & new closing checklist

Root Cause Isolated
Finding ID: DEEP-01 Certainty: VERIFIED (Calculated from Timecards & Rosters) Targeted Metric: Store Labor Cost (28.2% → 22.5%) Area: Closing Shift Execution
WHAT EXPLANATIONS WE TESTED & WHAT WAS RULED OUT
RULED OUT BY DATA

Explanation 1: Sales Slump

Net sales held steady at $310,000+ with guest check counts within 0.8% of historical baseline. Lower sales did not mathematically inflate labor percentage.

RULED OUT BY DATA

Explanation 2: Peak Dinner Rush Overstaffing

Dinner sales per labor hour (SPLH) averaged $86.20. Line cooks clocked out promptly within 15 minutes of orders dropping. Overtime did not occur on dinner shifts.

CONFIRMED ROOT CAUSE

Explanation 3: Closing Shift Procedures

100% of the labor increase concentrated after 9:30 PM. Closing shifts expanded from 1.1h to 2.4h due to dual manager overlap and a new 14-step cleaning procedure.

WHAT WE FOUND (THE ROOT CAUSE)
The increase in labor is concentrated in closing shifts and is associated with manager overlap plus longer closing procedures after a menu change. Specifically:
  • Two managers (one salaried GM and one hourly shift supervisor) were scheduled to close together every night, adding 18 redundant supervisory hours weekly.
  • A 14-step cleaning checklist instituted after the menu refresh required line cooks to deep-clean cold drawers nightly rather than on rotation, trapping 4 cooks on the clock until 1:15 AM.
WHY IT MATTERS
The restaurant was spending $4,850/month in non-revenue labor after the doors were locked. Adding more daytime staff or cutting dinner rush cooks would have harmed guest service without fixing the actual leak.

CORRECTIVE TEST & SUCCESS METRIC
• Corrective Test: Stagger closing managers (hourly supervisor clocks out at 10:00 PM; sole closing manager handles cash-out and final lockup). Streamline kitchen closing checklist to daily essentials (45 min) and move drawer deep-cleans to Tuesday morning prep shifts.
