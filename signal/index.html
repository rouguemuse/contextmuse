I // MARKET POSITIONING

Restaurants already have dashboards. Signal is built for the step after reporting.

Independent restaurants, multi-unit groups, and hospitality managers are rarely short on reporting. They drown in POS shift summaries, labor percentage reports, discount spreadsheets, and schedule audits that live in isolated tabs and never speak to each other.

Signal asks what happens when signals from those dashboards intersect, which findings are financially meaningful, which are trustworthy, and which are actually worth acting on.

A beautifully analyzed list of 25 problems is still 25 problems. Signal is designed to reduce the list.

CRITICAL POSITIONING

The difference between numbers, trends, and operational decisions.

Most restaurant management software ends at retrospective reporting. Signal begins where traditional software stops: translating operational drift into prioritized interventions.

A NORMAL REPORT TELLS YOU:
“Labor is 22%.”

A single, isolated historical metric. It records what happened in gross terms, but gives no operational context, daypart timing, or concurrent kitchen bottlenecks.

A BETTER DASHBOARD TELLS YOU:
“Labor increased 2.4 points.”

A directional trend line. It establishes that drift occurred over time, but leaves the operator guessing where it leaked, why it occurred, and what else broke simultaneously.

SIGNAL AIMS TO ANSWER:
“The increase is concentrated in Friday/Saturday 6–8 PM dinner rushes, overlaps a 68% void spike and 18% throughput drop, represents ~$1,500/mo exposure, and testing station deployment has lower friction than adding another shift employee.”

Intersectional, prioritized, epistemically verified, and friction-weighted before modifying operations.

EPISTEMIC SEPARATION

Separating evidence from hypothesis.

Every explanation in Signal separates undisputed facts from operational inferences. Signal strictly avoids unsupported causal assertions. Instead, the system reports what the submitted data directly establishes, what the pattern is consistent with, and what requires operator verification on the floor.

01 // OBSERVED

Direct Evidence

What the submitted records directly establish as empirical facts: timestamps, check amounts, void totals, roster hours, and modifier logs that exist without interpretation.

02 // INFERRED

Operational Pattern

What the combined evidence reasonably suggests. Statistical overlaps, temporal clusters, and recurring correlations labeled with explicit confidence ratings (Low, Moderate, High).

03 // VERIFY

Operator Floor Check

What needs to be checked by an operator before accepting the explanation. Targeted, 15-minute floor audits to confirm whether operational reality matches the data pattern.

04 // ACTION CANDIDATE

Targeted Remedy

What may be worth changing if real-world validation supports it. Specific, low-friction adjustments to shift prep, line pacing, or POS button configuration.

IV // FLAGSHIP OPERATIONAL AUDIT

Uncovering promotion economics hidden inside standard discount reports.

Discount reports usually show only three things: redemption counts, total discount dollars, and the sales volume attached to the promo code. But they fail to answer the single question that matters to the bottom line:

“Is the additional discount expense from an uncapped promotion producing enough incremental value to justify itself?”

The Analytical Nuance: Signal does not automatically conclude that the capped $5 offer is superior simply because it is cheaper. Signal evaluates whether the uncapped 10% offer meaningfully increases average check size, changes menu mix, improves guest conversion, or attracts higher-value orders—versus simply giving away margin on orders customers would have placed anyway.
WHY THIS EXAMPLE MATTERS

The gap between discount totals and commercial intelligence.

A TRADITIONAL REPORT SAYS:
“Promotion A generated $4,200 in discounts. Promotion B generated $7,900.”

Shows total discount expense in isolated silos. No connection to check size distribution, party size, or add-on pacing.

A BETTER DASHBOARD SAYS:
“Promotion B costs $3,700 more than Promotion A.”

Identifies the arithmetic difference between the two programs, but leaves the operator blind as to whether that $3,700 bought profitable revenue.

SIGNAL ASKS:
“What did the restaurant receive in exchange for that additional $3,700, and was it worth buying?”

Evaluates whether uncapped discounts drove incremental check expansion or subsidized regular dining habits.

LEVEL 2 // DIAGNOSTIC DEEP DIVE • COMPETING HYPOTHESIS AUDIT

Diagnostic Question: Does uncapped 10% create incremental revenue, or subsidize existing spend?

SYNTHETIC EXAMPLE • MULTI-SOURCE ANALYSIS
COMPETING OPERATIONAL HYPOTHESES (TESTING ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS)
HYPOTHESIS A
The 10% Offer Drives Larger Checks
EVIDENCE: WEAK / MODERATE

Redeemed 10% checks show average gross spend of $78.20 vs $74.50 for $5-off. However, the +$3.70 spend gain is fully consumed by the +$3.90 added discount cost, producing negative net revenue per redemption.

HYPOTHESIS B
Attracts Higher-Value Guests Who Would Spend More Anyway
EVIDENCE: MODERATE / STRONG

Guest party sizes and entrée counts on 10% redemptions mirror regular full-fare weekend guests, suggesting self-selection by high-spending diners rather than promo-driven expansion.

HYPOTHESIS C
Changes Menu Mix to High Margin
EVIDENCE: WEAK

High-margin add-ons (appetizers, desserts, specialty drinks) do not increase on 10% checks. The extra dollar volume comes from premium steak and wine bottles where the absolute discount is largest.

HYPOTHESIS D (STRONGEST OPERATIONAL FIT)
The Uncapped Structure Is Unnecessary Above $75
EVIDENCE: STRONG

82% of all promo redemptions occur between $50 and $85, where the dollar variance between $5 and 10% is under $3.50. The uncapped discount only creates substantial margin loss on checks above $100, where guests have already decided to dine regardless of the promotion.

What do I get at each level?

What reports and data does each level require?

Level 0 requires only 1–2 standard reports you already have (30–60d sales summary, hourly sales, or voids). Level 1 adds labor and menu mix data. Level 2 integrates check-level detail, timecards, modifier logs, and kitchen pacing timestamps. You can upload exports yourself or provide temporary reporting-only access.

What kinds of questions can each level answer?

Level 0 answers “Is there something in my data worth looking at?” Level 1 answers “Where should I look first?” Level 2 answers “Why is it happening, what is it costing, and what is the least disruptive useful thing to test first?” Ongoing Review answers “Did the change actually work?”

What does the Diagnostic discover that the Snapshot cannot?

The Snapshot establishes which problems matter most. The Diagnostic investigates competing explanations (e.g. testing whether a rush slowdown is caused by understaffing, kitchen line bottlenecks, or menu friction), explicitly distinguishes correlation from causation, and designs targeted floor experiments before you alter staffing.

Why would I pay for the deeper Diagnostic?

Because in a working restaurant, acting on the wrong explanation can be far more expensive than the problem itself. Adding a cook to solve a throughput problem can overcrowd the line and inflate payroll without fixing ticket pacing. The Diagnostic models friction and evidence strength before you spend money altering procedures.
